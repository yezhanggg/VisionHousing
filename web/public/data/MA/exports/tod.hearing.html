Hearing brief: Transit-oriented density, Massachusetts

Decision support, not a forecast. Not an official state document.

The question

Over 2027–2031, how many more homes would Massachusetts likely authorize by building permit with transit-oriented density, compared with today's rules?

The answer, as a likely range

Over 2027–2031, Massachusetts would likely permit between 3.5 and 3,900 more homes (0.0051–6.4%) with more homes allowed near major transit stops, on top of a baseline of 31,000–110,000 homes expected under today's rules.

What the headline counts

  • Transit-oriented density: the headline counts comparable. It is the Census-comparable figure.

Main drivers

  • Capitalisation rate used to value a finished mid-rise building: about 3,900 homes at its low value (4.5% a year), about 0.64 at its high value (7% a year).
  • Rent of a new mid-rise apartment as a multiple of the tract's two-bedroom median: about 74 homes at its low value (×1.05), about 3,700 at its high value (×1.5).
  • Construction cost per square foot, mid-rise apartment building: about 3,600 homes at its low value ($230/sq ft), about 4.9 at its high value ($400/sq ft).

What is unknown

  • Capitalisation rate used to value a finished mid-rise building: 15% of the uncertainty.
  • Construction cost per square foot, mid-rise apartment building: 14% of the uncertainty.
  • Rent of a new mid-rise apartment as a multiple of the tract's two-bedroom median: 12% of the uncertainty.
  • None: this reform creates no home by addition or conversion.
  • Unavailable: no completion series is read for this state, so no observed completion is reported.
  • Not modelled: no completion lag is applied, so no permit is turned into a delivered home here. The authorized net change if completed is the upper limit of what these permits could add to the stock.
  • No mechanism in this reform pushes a permit past 2031; a start delay lowers the total instead (permits not issued are not moved).
  • The survey covers places that issue building permits; a place with no permit requirement is outside it. The Census Bureau imputes figures for permit offices that did not report, and since 2023 it updates the set of permit offices every year. The baseline projects the published figures, imputations included.
  • Local rules not yet read (unresolved): 100% of homes near a major transit stop; the engine uses its statewide fallback range there.

What the model cannot tell you

  • Whether transit service near the stops will keep its current frequency.
  • Which station areas have land that could be rebuilt.
  • Where the baseline itself has been least accurate in past years: urban counties (where it has tended to run too low) and counties with a lower share of Qualified Census Tracts (where it has tended to run too low). Read the change in those places with extra care; the Method page shows the back-test.
  • The baseline repeats recent permit trends under today's rules. It does not predict a recession or a boom.
  • These are ranges of building permits, not a forecast of homes finished.
  • A reform's likely range is a simulation range: it shows how the result moves with the assumptions, given the baseline. No reform of this kind has past totals to check it against.
