# Technical Appendix to the Model and Assumptions Overview

This appendix holds the detail behind the [Model and Assumptions Overview](MODEL_AND_ASSUMPTIONS.md): the full Assumption Ledger tables, the baseline method in full, the transit rules, the uncertainty and cost accounting, the validation work, and the notes for each state beyond Pennsylvania. The Overview is the submitted document; read it first. Section numbers here (A to G) are referred to from the Overview.

As in the Overview, every result and assumption value sits inside a block marked `<!-- generated: ... -->`, written by `scripts/gen_docs_tables.py`; `make docs` fails if a block is out of date.

## A. The full Assumption Ledger

Every entry in `assumptions/parameters.yaml`. Entries of kind *sourced* or *benchmark* first, then *judgement* and *calibrated* entries with their rationale and what evidence would change them. The Overview (section 2.3) shows the key entries and the counts by kind.

## B. The baseline in full

Summary and the contest table: Overview section 4.2.

- Data: Building Permits Survey, annual, by county and structure type (1 unit, 2 units, 3 to 4 units, 5 or more units), 1990 onward.
- Rule contest: seven simple rules are back-tested on the same past start years ("origins") and the same county cases: the 5-year average, the 3-year average, the median of 5 years, a damped trend, "shrink to place type" (a county's permits per home pulled toward its county type's rate, small counties pulled more), "top-down share" (the state's 5-year average times the county's 10-year share of state permits), and the simple average of the six. Each is scored on its typical signed error (does it run high or low?), its typical miss, how often its P10-P90 range caught the real total, and how wide that range is.
- Selection: the choosing rule was fixed in `assumptions/backtest.yaml` before any score was computed. Rules whose ranges caught the real total in 75% to 85% of cases are eligible (`contest.coverage_band`); among them the lowest typical miss is best; the simplest eligible rule within 5% of it wins (`baseline.selection_tolerance`). If no rule is eligible, the rule whose coverage is closest to 80% wins. Each case's range is built from other counties' errors at earlier origins only (whose five target years had been observed by then), with size classes worked out from the years up to each origin; place types are today's grouping. The choice of rule uses every origin at once, so it is in-sample; the ranges and corrections are tested out of time. Each state applies the rule to its own back-test. The Method screen shows every rule's scores, losers included.
- Interval: empirical. Apply the winner at every past origin and collect the ratios of actual to projected permits in the county's pool (size class, or place type when that does better). The middle value (P50) is the rule's own projection. P10 and P90 are the projection times the pool's 10th and 90th percentile ratios, each divided by the pool's median ratio, so the range keeps its shape around the projection.
- Pools tested out of time: for each past origin, the range uses only cases whose five target years had already been observed, so no later information leaks in. Size-class and place-type pools are compared this way; place-type pools (all counties pooled when a type has too few past cases, `min_group_cases` in `assumptions/backtest.yaml`) are used only if they lower the out-of-time error with coverage inside the band. The Method screen shows the error and coverage for all counties and for every kind of place.
- Out-of-time calibrated ranges (tested, not adopted). Once each case's range was built only from errors known at the time, every rule's P10-P90 range caught the real five-year total far less often than the 80% it aims for. A fix was written into `assumptions/backtest.yaml` (`oot_calibration`) and fingerprinted in `assumptions/preregistration.yaml` before it was scored: for each past start year, rank the ratios of actual to projected totals of the cases already observed by then (the county's own included), in the county's size class (all counties when the class has fewer than 30), and take the finite-sample 10th and 90th percentile ranks as the range around the rule's own projection. It is adopted only if the four test states' coverage, pooled, lands in 75% to 85% and each state reaches at least 70%; above 85% it is not adopted either, and the levels are not re-tuned. The contest's choosing rule was re-run unchanged with the calibrated coverage. The result is below: the calibration widens the ranges and catches more real totals, but nowhere near enough, so the engine keeps today's ranges. The honest reading is that a likely range built from past errors catches the real total well under 80% of the time when a new housing cycle arrives, and the Method screen's findings and the baseline's limits say so. Method: [`docs/modules/baseline.md`](modules/baseline.md).

## C. Transit-oriented density: stops, distances and variants

### C.1 Which stops, and how far by foot

The benchmark preset keeps the simple stop rule of the Overview (section 2.2): a rail station, or a bus stop with a set number of weekday departures, measured in a straight line. The pack also carries a stricter, more realistic definition for the TOD variants (table `transit_walk_tract`, method in [`docs/modules/transit_walk.md`](modules/transit_walk.md)):

- **Major stop.** Every rail (fixed-guideway) stop. A bus stop only when buses leave the same platform side (all routes added) every 15 minutes or better on average in **both** weekday peaks, 7-9 am and 4-6 pm (at least 8 departures in each window on that side; a commuter-only stop busy one way in the morning and the other way in the afternoon does not qualify). A pole is split by side only where it serves both directions (`tod.frequent_bus_opposite_heading_deg`); a station is frequent when any one of its platforms is. The day is the feed's pinned representative weekday when the current feed still runs it, else its busiest Tuesday-Thursday; the date is published per feed in `transit_walk_county`. Ledger: `tod.frequent_bus_max_headway_min`, `tod.frequent_bus_am_*`, `tod.frequent_bus_pm_*`; rules `tod.walk.day_rule`, `tod.walk.direction_rule`, `tod.walk.headway_measure`.
- **Distance.** A **network walkshed** of 1/2 mile (804.7 m) on the OpenStreetMap street and path network, with 1/4 and 3/4 mile as sensitivities. Half a mile is the distance most transit riders walk to rail and the usual line in state transit-density laws; a network measure stops it from crossing rivers, rail yards and highways. Overlapping walksheds are dissolved, so land near two stops counts once.
- **Eligible land.** Streets and paths reached within the distance, widened by 50 m on each side (`tod.walk_edge_buffer_m`, about one lot depth), cut to the straight-line circle, then cut by tract outlines.
- **Straight-line fallback.** Where the network is incomplete (a stop with no walkable node within 80 m, `tod.walk_snap_m`; a stop on a network piece shorter than one mile, `tod.walk_min_component_m`; or a map tile that could not be fetched), the stop's straight-line circle is used and its area is reported separately (`fallback_acres_*`), so the result can be shown with and without it.
- **Eligible land is not capacity.** The walkshed is land within reach; how much of it could hold a mid-rise building is the scenario's developable share (`tod.developable_share_*`).

Each build reports, per county and per state, how many stops pass each rule (rail, frequent bus under the peak rule, bus under the daily benchmark rule), what share of the straight-line area the network walksheds reach at each distance, how many stops fell back to a straight line, and which counties have no keyless schedule (their buses never count as major). These figures are written to the pack's `transit_walk_county` table and summarised in [`docs/modules/transit_walk.md`](modules/transit_walk.md); they are not repeated here so that the document never drifts from the build. Stations hemmed in by rivers, rail yards and highways reach much less land on foot than a circle suggests.

### C.2 Benchmark presets, refinements and named variants

The five benchmark presets stay exactly as the Handbook defines them. Two kinds of upgrade sit beside them, each with ledger ids:

- **Refinements** change how the benchmark is measured, not what it is. ADUs: in counties whose municipal rules are read, lots count by legal-status category (already permitted, by right with conditions, discretionary, prohibited, unknown), weighted by single-family lots, so only lots where the benchmark changes the status quo are exposed (`adu.conditions_exposure_share`). Records added without changing any headline: ADUs by form (detached, semi-detached), full installed cost and the owner's cash; the missing-middle prototype comparison (duplex, fourplex, six-unit building, with code and financing thresholds); parking spaces before and after in matched prototypes, and surface or structured bounds; the fee cut's fiscal split (waived on homes built anyway, waived on added homes, paid by added homes, replacement funding, net change); TOD straight-line disclosure per county.
- **Named variants** (`assumptions/variants.yaml`) answer one question each on the same draws: tight or easy homeowner financing and full installed cost (ADU); the most profitable prototype per lot (missing middle); what builders would provide anyway, no response, all surface, all structured (parking); fee deferral instead of a cut (fees); 1/4, 1/2 and 3/4-mile network walksheds and a 3–4 storey walk-up (TOD), with a decomposition of why a state's TOD estimate is zero.

Methods: [ADU](methods/adu.md), [missing middle](methods/missing_middle.md), [parking](methods/parking.md), [fees](methods/fees.md), [TOD](methods/tod.md); the variant mechanism: [engine](modules/engine.md#named-variants-variantspy-assumptionsvariantsyaml).

## D. Packages, uncertainty and cost

### D.1 Packages and overlap

- A lot is used once. ADUs and missing middle compete for the same lots.
- Lots near transit are counted under the denser reform.
- Parking and fee savings enter the pro formas of the other reforms.
- The result is shown as: sum of parts, minus overlap, plus interaction, equals combined.
- **Interaction accounting** (on common draws): the package minus the sum of the reforms alone, per draw, for the state and each county; each reform's last-in effect (all five minus the other four) and interaction share (last-in minus alone); an ordered allocation in a stated order (the Handbook's numbering) whose credits add up to the package in every draw; and the named mechanisms with sentences written by rule. Method: [`docs/methods/comparisons.md`](methods/comparisons.md), section 1.

### D.2 Uncertainty, paired differences and public cost

- Every input has a low, central and high value and a shape.
- Monte Carlo draws, seeded so each run repeats exactly.
- Baseline and reform effect are drawn together.
- Results are shown as P10, P50 and P90, in words: "likely between ... and ...". The same range is also written as a frequency: "In eight of ten simulated futures, between X and Y homes". Eight in ten draws fall between the 10th and the 90th percentile, so the two wordings say the same thing; the frequency form is easier to read for many people.

**Paired differences.** In each draw the baseline and every reform are worked out from the same sampled inputs, including the same position of the baseline in its likely range. A reform's added homes in one draw are therefore "with the reform" minus "without it" in the same simulated future. The future level of building is very uncertain (the baseline range is wide), but most of that uncertainty is shared by the two sides and cancels in the difference. The engine reports, for every reform:

- the chance that the reform adds at least N homes, for each threshold N in the ledger (`explain.chance_threshold_1_units` to `_4_units`): the share of draws in which the state total is N or more;
- added homes as a share of the baseline, per draw, for the state and each county (P10, P50, P90);
- the width (P90 minus P10) of the baseline and of the added homes, and a sentence written by rule: "The future level of building is uncertain; the reform's effect on it is much less so" when the effect's width is under a quarter of the baseline's (`explain.paired_much_less_ratio`), "less so" when it is under the baseline's, and "about as uncertain" otherwise.

**What each reform costs.** A fee cut is the only benchmark reform with a direct public cost, and it applies to every permitted home, not only the extra ones. In each draw and county:

- revenue given up = fee cut per unit × (baseline homes + added homes);
- cost per added home = revenue given up ÷ added homes.

County revenue adds up to the state in every draw. Cost per added home is summarised over the draws where the reform adds homes; if it adds none in any draw the tool says "not defined" rather than showing a division by zero. Most of the revenue goes to homes that would have been built anyway, so the cost per added home is many times the cut per home. Two variants are shown:

- *State fee level.* The benchmark's $50,000 per home is replaced by `fees.pa_assumed_fee_per_unit_judgement`. No public source gives a statewide average of Pennsylvania's impact, tap and permit fees per new home (local tap, impact and permit fee schedules vary widely from town to town), so this entry is a labelled draft judgement with a wide range, and the variant says so.
- *Targeted cut.* The cut is limited to one group of similar counties (the "places like this one" groups). The fee cut works county by county, so the targeted cut's added homes are the same counties' added homes, and no new run is needed.

The parking reform has no public cost; builders save spaces avoided × cost per space on every home the reform reaches (both from the ledger, exactly as the parking reform used them). ADUs, missing middle and transit-oriented density have no direct public cost; the administrative cost of permits and inspections, and for transit-oriented density the cost of added water, sewer and transit capacity, is not modelled.

### F.2 Evidence from California's approval pipeline

Pennsylvania publishes no statewide record of applications, approvals and completions, so the model's timing and drop-out assumptions cannot be checked at home. California can: every city and county files a Housing Element Annual Progress Report (APR), and the state publishes it as open data. The command `visionhousing validate-california` reads it and writes three generated files. Method and terms: `docs/modules/validation_california.md`.

| File | What it holds | How it may be used |
|---|---|---|
| `assumptions/evidence/california_apr.yaml` | Comparison records (what changed, where, when, outcome, denominator, horizon, identification quality, caveats); stage tables; ADU permits per 1,000 one-unit detached homes by year | Evidence and validation only; not a model input |
| `assumptions/calibration/apr_timing_donor_ca.yaml` | Low-high ranges, across the state and the chosen cities, of days from application to building permit and from permit to completion, and the share reaching each stage within one to five years, by project group | **Transferred**: a timing or financing scenario may borrow them for a sensitivity, labelled "transferred from California" |
| `assumptions/calibration/adu_rate_plausibility.yaml` | The Pennsylvania ADU result as ADUs per 1,000 eligible lots per year, next to California's observed rates | The plausibility check on the ADU reform |

How it works, in short:

- **Stages.** Table A lists applications with their submission date. Table A2 lists entitlement, building permit and completion dates. An application is followed to the first later date of each stage on the same parcel, in the same jurisdiction, with the same unit category.
- **Groups kept apart.** ADUs, SB 9 duplexes and lot splits, streamlined or ministerial projects of five or more homes, other 2–4 and 5+ projects, and single-family homes are separate groups, decided by a fixed order of rules.
- **Projects still waiting.** Many projects have not reached the next stage when the data end. They are right-censored and handled with a Kaplan-Meier estimate, so recent applications neither count as failures nor get dropped.
- **Choosing places.** The cities compared were chosen by a rule written down first: large cities, two named for a documented difference in their ADU pathway, each matched to a peer with a similar ADU permit rate in 2018–2019. The choice step sees only those earlier years. A city with no close peer is shown as a plain before-and-after.
- **Strength of each comparison.** Every record says whether it is descriptive (before and after, or a plain comparison) or matched (against a peer chosen on its earlier trend). None is causal.

What this evidence cannot do: the reports are self-reported and not verified by the state; applications that cannot be joined to a later stage count as not reaching it, so reached shares are lower bounds; and California's high rents and strong state ADU law make its rates an upper reference for Pennsylvania, not a target.

### F.3 Results by legislative district

Results can also be read by state House and Senate district (`docs/modules/districts.md`). District totals are built in every draw from tract and county results, weighted by where the 2020 Census counted homes. A district that cuts across many tracts or counties is flagged: its number rests on the weights more than on its own data.

### F.4 Sensitivity methods in detail

- **Tornado.** Each input is swung from low to high with the others held central. Inputs are ranked by how much the state total moves.
- **Joint check.** Rank correlation between sampled inputs and the result confirms the order when inputs move together.
- **What would narrow the range.** For each reform's state total, each input's first-order share of the variance, from the run's own draws (no new run): sort the draws by the input, cut them into ten bins with the same number of draws (`sensitivity.variance_bins`), and compute 1 − (average variance inside a bin ÷ total variance). The share is the part of the spread that would go away if that input were known exactly. The likely range scales with the standard deviation, so knowing the input would shrink the range by about 1 − √(1 − share). The shares do not add up to one; the leftover is reported as "interactions and noise". The tool writes a short research agenda for the top inputs (`explain.agenda_inputs`): "Knowing X would shrink the range by about Y%". A permutation check would need one rerun per input, so it is not done; the rank correlations on the same draws are the cross-check.
- **Policy dials.** Each reform at 3-4 levels, one at a time, on the same seed; the benchmark level is the headline exactly. Readouts: "each step adds about X homes", the diminishing-return level, a target seeker ("which level reaches N homes", or "out of reach") and the ADU adoption threshold ("what annual take-up reaches N homes in five years"). A matrix crosses ADU take-up with construction cost (P50 and P10-P90 per cell).
- **Ranking stability.** The top inputs per reform (by rank correlation) are pinned at their low and high ends; the tool reports whether the order of the reforms changes and which pairs have overlapping likely ranges. Method: [`docs/methods/comparisons.md`](methods/comparisons.md), sections 2, 3 and 5.
- **Choices the user can change.** Cycle strength, transit distance, fee view, and each assumption's low, central or high value.

### G.2 Vermont: what broke, and which inputs fell back

**Why Vermont.** It is small (14 counties, 193 tracts) and has none of the local data Pennsylvania has: no town zoning rules read, no county parcel files, no pending bills read (one state statute is read; see below). It is thin even in some national files: BLS withholds its statewide construction pay. Other national files are about as complete as Pennsylvania's (see the table below). It still has bus feeds and a few frequent bus stops, so all five reforms run, including transit-oriented density; that reform finds major stops but adds no homes at Vermont rents (Overview, section 3.4). (Wyoming was the other candidate; with almost no transit it would test the TOD reform only by its absence.)

**What broke, and what the code now does instead of failing.**

- *Construction pay withheld for the whole state.* The build stopped. Now the state figure is the job-weighted mean of the counties BLS publishes, and the report card says so (`docs/modules/prices.md`).
- *Nullable counts in a small state's tables* broke the "places like this one" grouping. Columns are now read as numbers first.
- *Calibrations measured only in Pennsylvania.* The ADU physical-fit table was measured on Pennsylvania lots. Another state now borrows it with a wider range (`assumptions/transfer.yaml`; the widened range is in the table below). It shows in the state's Assumption Ledger with the reason.
- *Already-legal shares were one state's table.* The loader read only Pennsylvania's town-rule index, so Vermont fell back to the ledger's national default, which assumes most lots are not yet open to ADUs. Each state can now have its own table (`load_legal_shares`). Vermont's, `assumptions/calibration/already_legal_vt.yaml`, comes from state law: 24 V.S.A. § 4412(1)(E) already makes one ADU with a single-family home a permitted use, and § 4412(1)(D) makes duplexes a permitted use wherever year-round homes are allowed (and up to four units where municipal sewer and water serve). It sets Vermont's `adu.already_legal_share` (it also set `missing_middle.sf_only_zoned_share` until the dated § 4412(1)(D) floor replaced that judged share in the missing-middle funnel; see docs/methods/missing_middle.md). Compliance has not been measured town by town, so the range is documented judgement that stop well short of "all lots" (see the file and the Vermont Assumption Ledger).
- *A small back-test gave a narrow baseline range.* Vermont's state back-test rests on few county-origins, and its range came out narrower than Pennsylvania's. The state range is now widened when the back-test has few cases (`small_sample` in `assumptions/backtest.yaml`; `docs/modules/baseline.md`); Pennsylvania has enough cases and is unchanged.
- *Pennsylvania bills* are offered only for Pennsylvania. Vermont gets the five benchmark reforms.

### G.3 Massachusetts: a state where the law has already moved

Massachusetts is the third state the method is set up for. Two things differ from Pennsylvania and Vermont, and both are read from the state's own records ([`docs/modules/ma_intake.md`](modules/ma_intake.md)):

- **ADUs are already allowed by right** in single-family districts under state law (M.G.L. c. 40A § 3, as amended in 2024). So the ADU benchmark counts only what it adds beyond that law: larger units than the statute's size cap allows, no parking space, lots outside single-family districts, Boston, and towns whose rules lag the statute. That share is a documented judgement range, not a measurement (`assumptions/calibration/already_legal_ma.yaml`), and it overrides `adu.already_legal_share` for Massachusetts runs only.
- **MBTA Communities (Section 3A) districts** and the state's ADU permit survey are read as evidence and context. They do not change any estimate. The state has no statewide two-to-six-unit law, so the missing-middle share is not replaced.
- **What is read by script, and what is not.** The 3A district atlas comes from the MassGIS map service. The state's 3A lists and the ADU survey are files on www.mass.gov, which refuses scripted requests; the build respects that and reads them only from a copy a person saved by hand. Without those copies the 3A status table holds only the towns the atlas shows as compliant, and the ADU survey table is left out; the build notes say so.
- **Permit records are thinner than they look.** Almost every Massachusetts county-year in the last five years includes Census Bureau estimates for towns that did not report, more than in Pennsylvania or Vermont (see the permits row of the report card). The baseline range rests on those estimates.
- **Transit.** MBTA and regional transit schedules are read under the MassDOT Developers License Agreement (MassDOT is acknowledged as the provider). Where an agency's only keyless schedule had expired when read, its bus stops count as frequency unknown, not frequent, so transit reach there is understated.
- **Transit-oriented density and 3A.** On land 3A districts already zone for multi-family homes, the TOD benchmark counts only the additional legal capacity: max(0, homes the FAR 2.5 prototype allows − homes the district already allows) on the same developable land, in gross homes. Existing capacity is the atlas parcels' modeled capacity (`F_L_M__`), split across tracts by area. 3A's 15-per-acre floor is a district-wide minimum, not comparable with the prototype's density on one lot, so it is not used. The older rule (a judgement share of district acres taken out, `tod.state_zoned_overlap_share`) is variant `tod_ma_acre_share`; the run and county notes give the existing and additional capacity.
- **Hazards and walksheds.** The pack carries the flood-zone, land-cover and slope shares ([`docs/modules/hazards.md`](modules/hazards.md)) and the network walksheds around major stops ([`docs/modules/transit_walk.md`](modules/transit_walk.md)), built with the same commands as for Pennsylvania. Neither changes a benchmark estimate: the benchmark TOD preset uses straight-line distance, and the hazard shares are read only by the optional practical capacity screen and the place profile. FEMA has no digital flood map for Berkshire and Franklin counties and most of Hampshire County, so flood shares there are blank (the capacity screen draws them from the county or state share, never zero). No verified water and sewer service table is measured for Massachusetts, so the capacity screen's sewer part is drawn entirely from its ledger range. MassDEP's estimated sewer service areas are now in the pack as their own evidence class ("estimated service"; 70.8% of 2020 homes inside an estimated area, placeholder circles flagged as uncertain), but no estimate reads them yet ([`docs/modules/ma_capacity.md`](modules/ma_capacity.md)).
- **Existing 3A capacity, measured.** The pack also holds the 3A compliance model's own unit capacity per tract (`mbta_3a_capacity_tract`: 535,164 gross units in 464 tracts of the 159 compliant towns, parcel capacity split across tracts by area; a district counts its parcels or its own total, never both). It is ready to replace the acre share above with `max(0, proposed − existing)` capacity; until that engine step lands, the benchmark still uses the acre share.

### G.4 Wyoming: no transit, patchy reporting

Wyoming is a sparse-data state with a different gap from Vermont. It runs with the same commands (`make data STATE=WY`) and no Wyoming-only code. Three things differ:

- **No transit schedules, no rail.** Wyoming has no fixed-route rail. Its town bus systems are in the National Transit Map, so their stops are known, but none of them has a schedule feed (GTFS) listed in the Mobility Database catalog. So no bus stop's weekday departures can be counted, and no stop can be shown to be a major transit stop. The report card says "No major transit stops in WY" and explains why. Departures are marked *missing* (no one published them), not *zero*, and transit access in a tract that holds such a stop is marked *missing* too, not a true zero. The run does not fail: transit-oriented density has no eligible land and adds no homes, and no tract counts as transit-rich in the parking reform. Read that zero as "not measurable with published schedules", not as proof that no stop runs often. A schedule feed from any town system would let the reform run there.
- **No state reform law.** Wyoming's zoning statutes (W.S. 15-1-601 to 15-1-611 for cities and towns, 18-5-201 to 18-5-208 for counties) let local governments zone; they do not require any district to allow an ADU or more than one home. ADU and zoning bills in the 2025 and 2026 sessions were not enacted. The snapshot is in `assumptions/law/states/wy.yaml`. With no state law to read in, Wyoming has no already-legal table, and its ADU and missing-middle runs use the national default ranges (`adu.already_legal_share`, `missing_middle.sf_only_zoned_share`). Some cities allow ADUs in their own codes; those local rules are not read.
- **Small counties and thin permit counts.** Several counties authorize only a handful of homes in five years, and some county-years are zero. The baseline range for such counties is wide in relative terms, and county results are less reliable than the state total. Census Bureau imputation for offices that did not report is less common in Wyoming than in Pennsylvania or Vermont (see the permits row of the Wyoming report card). The thinner part is coverage: building permits count only places with a permit office, and parts of rural Wyoming have none, so the baseline can understate homes built there.
