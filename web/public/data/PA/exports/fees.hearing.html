Hearing brief: Development fee reduction, Pennsylvania

Decision support, not a forecast. Not an official state document.

The question

Over 2027–2031, how many more homes would Pennsylvania likely authorize by building permit with development fee reduction, compared with today's rules?

The answer, as a likely range

Over 2027–2031, Pennsylvania would likely permit between 2,700 and 13,000 more homes (2.3–8.6%) with development fees cut in half, on top of a baseline of 71,000–190,000 homes expected under today's rules.

What the headline counts

  • Development fee reduction: the headline counts comparable. It is the Census-comparable figure.

Main drivers

  • Share of a fee cut that reaches the decision to build: about 740 homes at its low value (5%), about 15,000 at its high value (100%).
  • Medium-run supply response to a change in profit per unit: about 2,200 homes at its low value (×0.3), about 15,000 at its high value (×2).
  • Baseline housing cycle (permits at the low or high end of their likely range): about 3,500 homes with permits at the low end of their range, about 9,300 at the high end.

What is unknown

  • Share of a fee cut that reaches the decision to build: 31% of the uncertainty.
  • Baseline housing cycle (permits at the low or high end of their likely range): 29% of the uncertainty.
  • Medium-run supply response to a change in profit per unit: 25% of the uncertainty.
  • None: this reform creates no home by addition or conversion.
  • Unavailable: no completion series is read for this state, so no observed completion is reported.
  • Not modelled: no completion lag is applied, so no permit is turned into a delivered home here. The authorized net change if completed is the upper limit of what these permits could add to the stock.
  • No mechanism in this reform pushes a permit past 2031; a start delay lowers the total instead (permits not issued are not moved).
  • The survey covers places that issue building permits; a place with no permit requirement is outside it. The Census Bureau imputes figures for permit offices that did not report, and since 2023 it updates the set of permit offices every year. The baseline projects the published figures, imputations included.
  • Local rules not yet read (unresolved): 99.8% of residents; the engine uses its statewide fallback range there.

What the model cannot tell you

  • Whether towns would replace lost fee revenue with other charges.
  • Whether roads, pipes or schools paid for by fees would be delayed, which could slow building.
  • How the saving is shared between landowners, builders and buyers in any one place.
  • The baseline repeats recent permit trends under today's rules. It does not predict a recession or a boom.
  • These are ranges of building permits, not a forecast of homes finished.
  • A reform's likely range is a simulation range: it shows how the result moves with the assumptions, given the baseline. No reform of this kind has past totals to check it against.
