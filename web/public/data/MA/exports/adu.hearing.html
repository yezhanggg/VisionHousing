Hearing brief: Accessory dwelling units, Massachusetts

Decision support, not a forecast. Not an official state document.

The question

Over 2027–2031, how many more homes would Massachusetts likely authorize by building permit with accessory dwelling units, compared with today's rules?

The answer, as a likely range

Over 2027–2031, Massachusetts would likely permit between 910 and 5,000 more homes (1.3–10%) with accessory dwelling units (ADUs) allowed, on top of a baseline of 31,000–110,000 homes expected under today's rules.

What the headline counts

  • Accessory dwelling units: the headline counts all work types. Census-comparable (Building Permits Survey definition): 523 units to 3,010 units, middle 1,390 units.

Main drivers

  • Share of feasible lots that add an ADU each year: about 900 homes at its low value (0.3% a year), about 7,500 at its high value (2.5% a year).
  • Construction cost of a new detached ADU per square foot: about 5,200 homes at its low value ($175/sq ft), about 1,000 at its high value ($375/sq ft).
  • Share of eligible lots where an ADU is already effectively legal: about 4,500 homes at its low value (55%), about 1,800 at its high value (82%).

What is unknown

  • Share of feasible lots that add an ADU each year: 30% of the uncertainty.
  • Construction cost of a new detached ADU per square foot: 19% of the uncertainty.
  • Share of eligible lots where an ADU is already effectively legal: 6.2% of the uncertainty.
  • Bound: how many of these a local permit system records, and how many a survey respondent reports as new construction, is unknown. Read the line as the most the reform could add outside the survey definition, not a measured count.
  • Unavailable: no completion series is read for this state, so no observed completion is reported.
  • Not modelled: no completion lag is applied, so no permit is turned into a delivered home here. The authorized net change if completed is the upper limit of what these permits could add to the stock.
  • No mechanism in this reform pushes a permit past 2031; a start delay lowers the total instead (permits not issued are not moved).
  • The survey covers places that issue building permits; a place with no permit requirement is outside it. The Census Bureau imputes figures for permit offices that did not report, and since 2023 it updates the set of permit offices every year. The baseline projects the published figures, imputations included.
  • Local rules not yet read (unresolved): 100% of single-family lots; the engine uses its statewide fallback range there.

What the model cannot tell you

  • Whether an ADU fits on a given lot: the fit test is a share for each census tract, not a site plan.
  • Septic limits, steep slopes, flood zones, historic districts and private deed rules, none of which are in the fit test.
  • Whether towns slow approvals or add new hurdles.
  • The baseline repeats recent permit trends under today's rules. It does not predict a recession or a boom.
  • These are ranges of building permits, not a forecast of homes finished.
  • A reform's likely range is a simulation range: it shows how the result moves with the assumptions, given the baseline. No reform of this kind has past totals to check it against.
