Hearing brief: All five benchmark reforms together, Vermont

Decision support, not a forecast. Not an official state document.

The question

Over 2027–2031, how many more homes would Vermont likely authorize by building permit with all five benchmark reforms together, compared with today's rules?

The answer, as a likely range

Over 2027–2031, Vermont would likely permit between 420 and 1,400 more homes (4.5–12%) with accessory dwelling units (ADUs) allowed, two to six homes allowed on single-family lots, more homes allowed near major transit stops, parking minimums removed and development fees cut in half, on top of a baseline of 5,100–18,000 homes expected under today's rules.

What the headline counts

  • Accessory dwelling units: the headline counts all work types. Census-comparable (Building Permits Survey definition): 41 units to 222 units, middle 103 units.
  • Missing middle: the headline counts comparable. It is the Census-comparable figure.
  • Transit-oriented density: the headline counts comparable. It is the Census-comparable figure.
  • Parking requirement reduction: the headline counts comparable. It is the Census-comparable figure.
  • Development fee reduction: the headline counts comparable. It is the Census-comparable figure.
  • Transit-oriented density: Partial footprint: 1,647 of 1,649 bus stops (99.8%) from 7 of 8 agencies have measured service.

Main drivers

  • Each reform alone, added up: 410 units to 1,400 units
  • Minus lots counted twice: 0.0015 units to 0.061 units
  • Plus savings that feed another reform: 2 units to 32 units
  • All reforms together: 420 units to 1,400 units

What is unknown

  • Share of feasible lots that add an ADU each year: 30% of the uncertainty.
  • Bound: how many of these a local permit system records, and how many a survey respondent reports as new construction, is unknown. Read the line as the most the reform could add outside the survey definition, not a measured count.
  • Unavailable: no completion series is read for this state, so no observed completion is reported.
  • Not modelled: no completion lag is applied, so no permit is turned into a delivered home here. The authorized net change if completed is the upper limit of what these permits could add to the stock.
  • No mechanism in this reform pushes a permit past 2031; a start delay lowers the total instead (permits not issued are not moved).
  • The survey covers places that issue building permits; a place with no permit requirement is outside it. The Census Bureau imputes figures for permit offices that did not report, and since 2023 it updates the set of permit offices every year. The baseline projects the published figures, imputations included.
  • Floor area per missing-middle unit: 8.9% of the uncertainty.
  • None: this reform creates no home by addition or conversion.
  • Capitalisation rate used to value a finished mid-rise building: 2.5% of the uncertainty.
  • Baseline housing cycle (permits at the low or high end of their likely range): 46% of the uncertainty.
  • Baseline housing cycle (permits at the low or high end of their likely range): 36% of the uncertainty.

What the model cannot tell you

  • Whether an ADU fits on a given lot: the fit test is a share for each census tract, not a site plan.
  • For-sale townhouses and condominiums: every new building is valued as a rental.
  • Whether transit service near the stops will keep its current frequency.
  • Whether lenders in a given market would finance buildings with fewer spaces.
  • Whether towns would replace lost fee revenue with other charges.
  • The baseline repeats recent permit trends under today's rules. It does not predict a recession or a boom.
  • These are ranges of building permits, not a forecast of homes finished.
  • A reform's likely range is a simulation range: it shows how the result moves with the assumptions, given the baseline. No reform of this kind has past totals to check it against.
