Hearing brief: Missing middle, Massachusetts

Decision support, not a forecast. Not an official state document.

The question

Over 2027–2031, how many more homes would Massachusetts likely authorize by building permit with missing middle, compared with today's rules?

The answer, as a likely range

Over 2027–2031, Massachusetts would likely permit between 78 and 2,600 more homes (0.12–4.7%) with two to six homes allowed on single-family lots, on top of a baseline of 31,000–110,000 homes expected under today's rules.

Main drivers

  • Share of feasible lots redeveloped each year: about 120 homes at its low value (0.05% a year), about 2,300 at its high value (1% a year).
  • Capitalisation rate used to value the finished building: about 2,200 homes at its low value (5% a year), about 88 at its high value (7.5% a year).
  • Rent premium of a new 2-6 unit building's homes over the census two-bedroom median: about 150 homes at its low value (×1.05), about 2,200 at its high value (×1.5).

What is unknown

  • Share of feasible lots redeveloped each year: 15% of the uncertainty.
  • Rent premium of a new 2-6 unit building's homes over the census two-bedroom median: 13% of the uncertainty.
  • Capitalisation rate used to value the finished building: 13% of the uncertainty.
  • None: this reform creates no home by addition or conversion.
  • Unavailable: no completion series is read for this state, so no observed completion is reported.
  • Not modelled: no completion lag is applied, so no permit is turned into a delivered home here. The authorized net change if completed is the upper limit of what these permits could add to the stock.
  • No mechanism in this reform pushes a permit past 2031; a start delay lowers the total instead (permits not issued are not moved).
  • The survey covers places that issue building permits; a place with no permit requirement is outside it. The Census Bureau imputes figures for permit offices that did not report, and since 2023 it updates the set of permit offices every year. The baseline projects the published figures, imputations included.
  • Local rules not yet read (unresolved): 100% of single-family lots; the engine uses its statewide fallback range there.

What the model cannot tell you

  • For-sale townhouses and condominiums: every new building is valued as a rental.
  • Which lots sit side by side, or which owners would sell.
  • The real price of a house bought to be torn down; census home values are owners' own estimates.
  • The baseline repeats recent permit trends under today's rules. It does not predict a recession or a boom.
  • These are ranges of building permits, not a forecast of homes finished.
  • A reform's likely range is a simulation range: it shows how the result moves with the assumptions, given the baseline. No reform of this kind has past totals to check it against.
