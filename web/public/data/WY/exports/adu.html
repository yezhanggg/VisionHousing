Housing reforms in Wyoming: homes permitted, 2027–2031

Decision support, not a forecast. Not an official state document.

In one sentence

Over 2027–2031, Wyoming would likely permit between 160 and 700 more homes (1.3–9.3%) with accessory dwelling units (ADUs) allowed, on top of a baseline of 4,900–18,000 homes expected under today's rules.

What moves this estimate most

Each input below is moved alone from its low to its high value while every other input stays at its central value. The figures are the five-year homes this reform adds at each end.

  1. Share of feasible lots that add an ADU each year: about 110 homes at its low value (0.3% a year), about 920 at its high value (2.5% a year).
  2. Share of fitting lots where an owner builds for family use regardless of rent: about 200 homes at its low value (1%), about 790 at its high value (15%).
  3. Construction cost of a new detached ADU per square foot: about 640 homes at its low value ($175/sq ft), about 250 at its high value ($375/sq ft).
  4. Position inside the calibrated ADU fit range: about 200 homes at its low value (×−1.5), about 490 at its high value (×1.5).
  5. Loan term for ADU financing: about 310 homes at its low value (15 years), about 480 at its high value (30 years).

What this cannot tell you

  • Whether an ADU fits on a given lot: the fit test is a share for each census tract, not a site plan.
  • Septic limits, steep slopes, flood zones, historic districts and private deed rules, none of which are in the fit test.
  • Whether towns slow approvals or add new hurdles.
  • How quickly owners take up the new right. This is the widest assumption and is not yet checked against places like this state.
  • Whether every ADU shows up in permit statistics. Many towns permit an ADU as an addition or a conversion, not as a new home, and the national permit survey does not count those, so some of the homes added here may never appear in published permit counts.
  • Where the baseline itself has been least accurate in past years: 'Rural, slow-growth, lower-cost' counties (where it has tended to run too high) and small counties (where it has tended to run too high). Read the change in those places with extra care; the Method page shows the back-test.

What these estimates cannot tell you

  • These are ranges of building permits, not a forecast of homes finished.
  • A reform's likely range is a simulation range: it shows how the result moves with the assumptions, given the baseline. No reform of this kind has past totals to check it against.
  • They describe the whole state. They do not say which lots, streets or owners would build.
  • They do not say who would live in the new homes or what they would pay.
  • They assume every town follows the new rule from the first day and approves permits at today's pace.
  • They say nothing about years after the five-year period.

The baseline

  • The baseline repeats recent permit trends under today's rules. It does not predict a recession or a boom.
  • The baseline range is not a promise. Tested on past years, using only what was known at the time, ranges built this way caught the real five-year total less often than eight times in ten for counties. Each range states how often ranges like it caught the real total.
  • Those past years hold about one housing cycle, and misses ran mostly to one side and moved together across counties, so the share caught rests on few independent periods.
  • The rule behind the range was chosen using every past year, including the years it was later scored on, and with today's county types rather than those known at the time, so the share caught is somewhat favourable. Each count's validation card names the study, years and area it comes from.
  • In every draw all counties sit at the same point of the housing cycle, and costs, credit and review speed are drawn on their own. A separate sensitivity shows the ranges when regions swing apart inside the state range and when high costs, tight credit and slow review come together; it does not replace these ranges.
  • Counties without a permit office, or with imputed permit counts, carry the Census Bureau's estimates.
  • The year between the last full year of permit data and the first projected year has no full-year count yet. The baseline carries the latest yearly level through it; a nowcast from the monthly files is shown as a check only, and a part-year count is never treated as a full year.
  • The middle value depends on which past years the rule averages. In Pennsylvania one unusually high year sits in the window; the baseline card shows the effect of leaving it out as a disclosed sensitivity, not a replacement.
  • In states with a short permit record, the range is widened toward the past errors of states that grew at a similar pace. Those errors come from one recovery after a housing bust, so the next cycle may differ, and the ranges still caught the real total less often than they aim to.
  • Where the baseline itself has been least accurate in past years: 'Rural, slow-growth, lower-cost' counties (where it has tended to run too high) and small counties (where it has tended to run too high). Read the change in those places with extra care; the Method page shows the back-test.

How the ranges are made

Every uncertain assumption has a low, central and high value in the Assumption Ledger. The model runs 2,000 times (seed 0), drawing each assumption from its range, and reports the spread of answers. Run it again with the same seed and every number comes out the same.

How the headline numbers are worked out

  • baseline permitted units 2027-2031, state: P50 = sum of county P50s = 10,090 units; range from the same rule (mean of 2023-2025 permits) backtested on state totals at 21 past origins (state point projection 10,090 units): P10 = P50 × 0.635 / 1.010, P90 = P50 × 1.779 / 1.010 (ratio P10, typical cycle q0.5, P90); few cases (475 county-origins, weight w = 475 / 1,000 = 0.47), so each end is blended toward the PA prior on the log scale (w x own + (1 - w) x prior) and only widened (assumptions/backtest.yaml, small_sample): P10 = P50 × 0.539, P90 = P50 × 1.761; growth-aware widening (growth class fast at 2025, 325 other-state cases, w = 0.47; each end is the wider of itself and w x end + (1 - w) x log(pool ratio), pool ratios P10 0.447 and P90 1.540): P10 = P50 × 0.489, P90 = P50 × 1.761 (assumptions/calibration/growth_coverage.yaml) = P10 4,934 units, P50 10,090 units, P90 17,764 units; measured reliability of the state range, shown as "range (caught 100% of past totals)": in past years, ranges like this caught the real five-year total in 8 of 8 cases (out of time) (assumptions/calibration/baseline_reliability.yaml)
  • added units authorized, Accessory dwelling units 2027-2031, state: P10, P50, P90 of 2,000 seeded draws of the sum over counties of added units per tract, summed to the county in each draw; per draw: the sum over tracts of added units authorized = ADUs authorized x (1 - displacement share x) (no home removed, so also the net added homes) = 158.23 units, 361.86 units, 699.21 units
  • combined added units, Accessory dwelling units 2027-2031, state: P10, P50, P90 of 2,000 seeded draws of sum of individual effects - overlap removed + interaction added, in each draw = 158.23 units, 361.86 units, 699.21 units

Technical notes from the model

  • Attached single-family lots are included at a much lower physical fit.
  • Each draw places the baseline at one position in its likely range, the same for every county; reforms acting on baseline production use the same position.
  • A lot is used once: when ADU and missing middle are both on, the lots the ADU reform builds on leave the missing-middle pool (missing_middle.adu_lot_overlap_share).
  • When the fee cut is on, the ADU and 2-6 unit pro formas' fee lines are cut by the same share; when parking reform is on, their parking lines are scaled by the share of spaces still built outside transit-rich tracts.
  • Fee and parking savings on baseline production are added; their cross term is not modelled.
