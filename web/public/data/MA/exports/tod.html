Housing reforms in Massachusetts: homes permitted, 2027–2031

Decision support, not a forecast. Not an official state document.

In one sentence

Over 2027–2031, Massachusetts would likely permit between 3.5 and 3,900 more homes (0.0051–6.4%) with more homes allowed near major transit stops, on top of a baseline of 31,000–110,000 homes expected under today's rules.

What moves this estimate most

Each input below is moved alone from its low to its high value while every other input stays at its central value. The figures are the five-year homes this reform adds at each end.

  1. Capitalisation rate used to value a finished mid-rise building: about 3,900 homes at its low value (4.5% a year), about 0.64 at its high value (7% a year).
  2. Rent of a new mid-rise apartment as a multiple of the tract's two-bedroom median: about 74 homes at its low value (×1.05), about 3,700 at its high value (×1.5).
  3. Construction cost per square foot, mid-rise apartment building: about 3,600 homes at its low value ($230/sq ft), about 4.9 at its high value ($400/sq ft).
  4. Floor area per mid-rise apartment: about 3,600 homes at its low value (700 sq ft), about 330 at its high value (1,000 sq ft).
  5. Share of feasible mid-rise sites permitted each year: about 420 homes at its low value (0.5% a year), about 2,900 at its high value (5% a year).

What these estimates cannot tell you

  • These are ranges of building permits, not a forecast of homes finished.
  • A reform's likely range is a simulation range: it shows how the result moves with the assumptions, given the baseline. No reform of this kind has past totals to check it against.
  • They describe the whole state. They do not say which lots, streets or owners would build.
  • They do not say who would live in the new homes or what they would pay.
  • They assume every town follows the new rule from the first day and approves permits at today's pace.
  • They say nothing about years after the five-year period.

The baseline

  • The baseline repeats recent permit trends under today's rules. It does not predict a recession or a boom.
  • The baseline range is not a promise. Tested on past years, using only what was known at the time, ranges built this way caught the real five-year total less often than eight times in ten for counties. Each range states how often ranges like it caught the real total.
  • Those past years hold about one housing cycle, and misses ran mostly to one side and moved together across counties, so the share caught rests on few independent periods.
  • The rule behind the range was chosen using every past year, including the years it was later scored on, and with today's county types rather than those known at the time, so the share caught is somewhat favourable. Each count's validation card names the study, years and area it comes from.
  • Counties without a permit office, or with imputed permit counts, carry the Census Bureau's estimates.
  • The year between the last full year of permit data and the first projected year has no full-year count yet. The baseline carries the latest yearly level through it; a nowcast from the monthly files is shown as a check only, and a part-year count is never treated as a full year.
  • The middle value depends on which past years the rule averages. In Pennsylvania one unusually high year sits in the window; the baseline card shows the effect of leaving it out as a disclosed sensitivity, not a replacement.
  • In states with a short permit record, the range is widened toward the past errors of states that grew at a similar pace. Those errors come from one recovery after a housing bust, so the next cycle may differ, and the ranges still caught the real total less often than they aim to.
  • Where the baseline itself has been least accurate in past years: urban counties (where it has tended to run too low) and counties with a lower share of Qualified Census Tracts (where it has tended to run too low). Read the change in those places with extra care; the Method page shows the back-test.

How the ranges are made

Every uncertain assumption has a low, central and high value in the Assumption Ledger. The model runs 2,000 times (seed 0), drawing each assumption from its range, and reports the spread of answers. Run it again with the same seed and every number comes out the same.

How the headline numbers are worked out

  • baseline permitted units 2027-2031, state: P50 = sum of county P50s = 66,715 units; range from the same rule (mean of 2023-2025 permits) backtested on state totals at 21 past origins (state point projection 66,715 units): P10 = P50 × 0.509 / 1.097, P90 = P50 × 1.648 / 1.097 (ratio P10, typical cycle q0.5, P90); growth-aware widening (growth class slow at 2025, 350 other-state cases, w = 0.29; each end is the wider of itself and w x end + (1 - w) x log(pool ratio), pool ratios P10 0.461 and P90 1.821): P10 = P50 × 0.462, P90 = P50 × 1.721 (assumptions/calibration/growth_coverage.yaml) = P10 30,812 units, P50 66,715 units, P90 114,793 units; measured reliability of the state range, shown as "range (caught 88% of past totals)": in past years, ranges like this caught the real five-year total in 7 of 8 cases (out of time) (assumptions/calibration/baseline_reliability.yaml)
  • added units authorized, Transit-oriented density 2027-2031, state: P10, P50, P90 of 2,000 seeded draws of the sum over counties of added units per tract, summed to the county in each draw; per draw: the sum over tracts of added units authorized = absorbed x net-new share (units moved within the region are not counted) = 3.5 units, 1,155 units, 3,903 units
  • combined added units, Transit-oriented density 2027-2031, state: P10, P50, P90 of 2,000 seeded draws of sum of individual effects - overlap removed + interaction added, in each draw = 3.5 units, 1,155 units, 3,903 units

Technical notes from the model

  • Distance: 1/2 mile (rail) or 1/4 mile (frequent bus). Net-new share (tod.net_new_share) is wide: homes moved within the region are not counted.
  • Parking near stops is already reduced inside the TOD pro forma (the transit-rich still-built share); the parking reform counts baseline production only, so no unit is counted twice.
  • Land Massachusetts already zones for multi-family homes by right (Section 3A parcels in the distance, atlas F_L_M__ split across tracts by area) counts only its additional legal capacity: max(0, homes the FAR 2.5 five-storey prototype allows - homes the 3A district already allows) on the same developable land, in gross homes (records existing_3a_*, proposed_3a_capacity, additional_3a_capacity). 3A's 15 homes per acre is a district-wide minimum gross density; the prototype is about 105 per acre on one lot: different measures, so the parcels' own modeled capacity is compared, not the 15-per-acre floor. Eligible land in tracts with no compliant 3A district in the atlas: 53%, existing 3A capacity 0 there (base-zoning rule); the pack cannot tie a tract to its town's 3A status. Variant tod_ma_acre_share keeps the older acre-share rule.
  • Transit status (state): discovered partial; legal yes; coverage partial; headway yes; headway peak yes; feasible yes. Footprint: partial footprint: 12,337 of 14,855 bus stops (83.0%) from 14 of 23 agencies have measured service.
  • Each draw places the baseline at one position in its likely range, the same for every county; reforms acting on baseline production use the same position.
  • A lot is used once: when ADU and missing middle are both on, the lots the ADU reform builds on leave the missing-middle pool (missing_middle.adu_lot_overlap_share).
  • When the fee cut is on, the ADU and 2-6 unit pro formas' fee lines are cut by the same share; when parking reform is on, their parking lines are scaled by the share of spaces still built outside transit-rich tracts.
  • Fee and parking savings on baseline production are added; their cross term is not modelled.
